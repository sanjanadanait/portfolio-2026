Unified Decryption Access

How I cut time-to-access for encrypted data from 9 days to 3 while preserving column-level governance.
Hero image coming soon
Role Lead Product Designer (user research + product design)
Timeline FY25, Q3 to Q4
Scope End-to-end: research, journey mapping, workflow design, steward and requestor experiences, MVP and post-MVP scoping
Partners Data Access, Data Lake, Data Security, key-owner teams, Legal / Compliance
Tools Figma, FigJam, Cursor, Claude

Overview

At Intuit, any data field classified as restricted or sensitive (most often PII) is encrypted at the application level, and about 10% of tables in the data lake contain columns that need decryption to be usable. That small percentage caused a disproportionate amount of pain for the analysts, engineers, and data scientists who depend on this data.

To actually use one of those tables, a user had to clear two entirely separate approvals, in two different tools: first request access to the table itself, then go through an unrelated process to request the decryption keys for its encrypted columns. The two systems didn't know about each other, so people would clear the first approval, start working, and only then discover a second gate they hadn't known existed. Start to finish, getting to usable data averaged 9 days.

I owned this project end to end, from the research that quantified the problem through the shipped design. The result unified the two approvals into a single request and cut time-to-access from 9 days to 3, without giving up any of the compliance control that governs encrypted data. This case study walks through how, and why the hardest parts were underneath the surface rather than on it.

The problem

The core issue was fragmentation between two approval systems that should have been one. Table access lived in Data Discovery. Decryption-key access lived in a separate process entirely. Nothing connected them, and nothing told a user at the start that a table even had encrypted columns, so the second gate was usually a surprise discovered mid-task.

The problem statement I worked from:

I am a data analyst trying to confirm whether a table can power my report, dashboard, or model, but it takes a long time to get usable access, because after requesting and receiving access to the table I find I have to go through a separate process to decrypt the columns I need, which makes me feel frustrated and inefficient.

The design goal followed directly: make getting to encrypted data feel like one request instead of two, without weakening any of the controls that make encryption meaningful in the first place. That second half is what made this hard. This is a domain where the analyst is not the only stakeholder. Legal, security, and the key-owner teams all have a legitimate say in who decrypts what, and a design that quietly loosened those controls to feel faster would have been a failure, no matter how clean it looked.

Research

I approached this with a mix of quantitative and qualitative work, and the qualitative side is what surfaced the real design challenge.

On the quantitative side, I reviewed query logs and Slack support-channel data to confirm the 9-day average was real and widespread, and analyzed encrypted-column data from the internal key-management system to understand how often and where decryption was needed. On the qualitative side, I ran follow-me-home sessions with analysts to watch the current process firsthand, and built a user journey diagram to map exactly where time was being lost. Throughout, I worked with two distinct sets of people: the data consumers, stewards, and key owners who live inside the flow, and the governance and legal/compliance stakeholders who define the rules the flow has to satisfy.

Four insights came out of that research. The first two confirmed the problem; the last two defined how hard it would be to solve well:

Insight 01 The journey was fragmented and high-friction. Exactly as the problem statement described: two disconnected approvals, in two different tools, with no signal at the start that a second gate even existed.
Insight 02 Encrypted data was growing fast. There had been roughly 360,000 calls to obtain encryption keys in a single month, and the org expected a 5x growth in encrypted data. Whatever I designed had to scale.
Insight 03 Keys and columns have a complex relationship. A single table can have multiple encrypted columns, and those columns can be protected by different keys with different owners.
Insight 04 Key ownership is genuinely complicated. The owner of a table is often not the owner of its decryption keys. In 80% of legacy cases the table owner and key owner were different, while about 90% of new requests going forward would have the same owner. The design had to handle both cleanly.

Exploration 1 (rejected): point users to the second process earlier

My first exploration was the obvious, low-effort one: highlight the encrypted columns in Discovery and add a message in the table request flow, with a link out to the separate decryption process. Show people the second door earlier so they aren't surprised by it.

I rejected it. It only provided better directions to a broken process rather than fixing it. It gave users earlier visibility but left the two systems just as disconnected, and modeling showed it would save only about two days. It didn't touch the core fragmentation, so it didn't deserve to ship.

Exploration 2 (shipped): one request, with compliance control preserved

The design that shipped collapsed the two approvals into a single in-flow request. When a user requests table access in Discovery, the flow now tells them how many columns are encrypted, lets them select the specific columns they need, and captures justification for that decryption need in the same step. They submit once and get a link to track status. On approval, key access is provisioned automatically with no further user action.

From the user's point of view it went from two processes to one, faster and simpler. The craft was in doing that without weakening a single compliance control, which meant handling the workflow complexity honestly rather than smoothing it over:

  • Column-level selection and justification. Users request only the specific encrypted columns they need and say why, which keeps access minimized to what's actually required rather than decrypting everything by default.
  • Partial approval by design. A steward or key owner can approve some columns and deny others, each denial carrying a justification the requestor sees. The outcome "you can have three of these columns but not the fourth, and here's why" is a first-class result of the flow, not an error state.
  • Splitting one request into many only when ownership requires it. When the table owner and key owner are the same (the ~90% forward case), the user gets a single request. When they differ (the 80% legacy case), the system generates separate requests to each owner behind the scenes, so each owner only approves what they're responsible for. The user still experiences one submission; the routing complexity is hidden.
  • A safe fallback when ownership is unknown. Key-ownership information didn't exist for every column. Rather than fail silently, the design detects that a column is encrypted and directs the requestor to reach out to the steward for guidance when it can't resolve the owner, so the user is never left at a dead end.

Scoping: MVP vs. post-MVP

I split delivery to get value out fast without compromising the foundation. The MVP was unified decryption access for tables, prioritized because it attacked the biggest bottleneck (the 9-day table case), delivered an immediate ~67% time-to-access reduction, and established the integrated workflow every future decryption capability would build on. The post-MVP extended the model to Kafka topics for real-time use cases and completed integration with the central request-tracking hub so all access status lives in one source of truth. One more decision worth noting: to avoid sync problems between the two underlying systems, all new requests were routed through a single system of record rather than trying to keep two systems in lockstep.

Impact

9 → 3 days To get access to tables with encrypted columns, a ~67% reduction.
2 → 1 Fragmented approval workflows collapsed into one integrated request.
360K+ Monthly decryption key requests supported, built to scale with the expected 5x growth in encrypted data.
One system Reduced security risk from untracked decryption by bringing every request into one governed, auditable system of record.

Reflection

01 The simplest surface often sits on top of the most complexity. Hiding that complexity from the user is the work, not a shortcut around it. The user experiences one request. Underneath it are column-level minimization, partial approval, owner-based routing, and a safe fallback for missing ownership data, none of which I was willing to drop to make the screen look cleaner.
02 The hardest design decisions weren't visual. They were about how to honor a compliance rule without passing its complexity on to the user, and most of them were worked out in conversations with the security, key-owner, and governance teams rather than in Figma. Shipping a genuinely simpler experience in a domain this sensitive depended as much on aligning those partners as on the interface itself.
03 The clearest next step is closing the loop with analytics. Tracking how often partial approvals happen, where users still hit the ownership-unknown fallback, and whether the single-request model holds up as encrypted data grows toward the projected 5x. That measurement would turn a strong launch into a continuously improving one.
